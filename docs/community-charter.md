# Research AI Commons
## Community Concept, Structure & Operating Principles
**Status:** Draft / Working Document  
**Purpose:** Community charter and source document for website development  
**Working name:** Research AI Commons

---

## 1. One-line concept

**Real research workflows, shared across labs.**

Research AI Commons is a researcher-led community for sharing how AI is actually used in research: what works, what fails, what feels risky, and how research workflows are changing across labs and disciplines.

---

## 2. Why this community exists

Generative AI is rapidly entering research workflows. It is already being used for literature search, writing, coding, data analysis, experimental planning, information organization, presentation preparation, and many other tasks.

At the same time, actual adoption is highly uneven.

Different laboratories and research fields have different practices, assumptions, risks, and barriers. Some researchers use AI every day, while others use it only for English editing or do not use it for research at all. Even when the same tools are used, the way they are incorporated into research can differ substantially.

There are already many lectures, tutorials, workshops, and institutional seminars about generative AI. However, fewer spaces focus on the **real, day-to-day research workflow**:

- How do researchers actually use AI at the bench?
- Which tasks are genuinely improved?
- What kinds of mistakes occur?
- What should not be entrusted to AI?
- How do researchers verify AI-generated outputs?
- How should unpublished data be handled?
- What prevents researchers from adopting useful tools?
- How do practices differ across laboratories and disciplines?

Research AI Commons is designed to fill this gap.

The community does not begin with the assumption that AI should be adopted everywhere. Instead, it creates a place where researchers can compare real practices, failures, concerns, and experiments with AI, and collectively develop better ways of using emerging technologies in research.

---

## 3. Core philosophy

### 3.1 Research comes first

The goal is not to promote a particular AI product or maximize AI usage.

AI is treated as one component of the research workflow. The starting point is always:

**What are researchers trying to accomplish, and where does AI genuinely help or hinder that work?**

---

### 3.2 Share practice, not just knowledge

The community values practical and contextual knowledge.

Examples include:

- “I used this workflow for literature screening.”
- “This analysis became faster, but verification took longer.”
- “This prompt produced a convincing but scientifically incorrect answer.”
- “Our lab does not upload unpublished data to external AI services.”
- “This coding workflow worked even for a researcher with limited programming experience.”

Successes and failures are equally valuable.

---

### 3.3 Across labs, across disciplines

The initial community may grow from experimental and life-science researchers, but the long-term goal is interdisciplinary.

Experimental researchers, computational researchers, information scientists, engineers, clinicians, humanities and social-science researchers, research-support staff, and others may approach AI differently.

Those differences are not a problem to eliminate; they are a source of insight.

---

### 3.4 Discussion before prescription

Research AI Commons is not primarily a place where experts teach the “correct” way to use AI.

It is a place where participants first compare what is actually happening in research environments.

Recommendations, guides, and best practices should emerge from discussion, evidence, experience, and expert input rather than being imposed at the beginning.

---

### 3.5 Open, but responsible

The community aims to share useful knowledge openly while respecting:

- unpublished research,
- confidential information,
- intellectual property,
- personal information,
- institutional policies,
- collaborators,
- publication and authorship rules.

Openness does not mean that all information should be disclosed.

---

## 4. Community structure

Research AI Commons consists of several complementary activity formats.

### 4.1 Research AI Café

**Purpose:** Casual, recurring peer-to-peer discussion.

The Café is the core community activity.

It is intended to be small, informal, and easy to organize. Researchers share how they are using AI, problems they have encountered, questions they have, and ideas they want to try.

Typical format:

- 10–20 participants
- 60–120 minutes
- short introductions or lightning talks
- small-group discussion
- open conversation
- optional informal networking afterward

The Café may be held regularly or irregularly depending on community activity.

It should remain lightweight enough that it can be organized repeatedly without requiring a major event-production effort.

#### Example topics

- How are you actually using AI in research?
- What do you use for literature search?
- Can AI help with experimental design?
- Where does AI-generated code become dangerous?
- What research data would you never upload?
- What tasks have you stopped doing manually?
- What AI workflow failed unexpectedly?
- How do different laboratories verify AI output?

---

### 4.2 Language format

Café sessions may be held in Japanese or English.

The language is treated as an attribute of each event rather than as a separate community or series.

Examples:

- **Research AI Café #001 | JP**
- **Research AI Café #002 | EN**
- **Research AI Café #003 | JP**
- **Research AI Café #004 | EN**

The event number functions as a unique chronological identifier.

This allows each event to be referenced consistently in:

- reports,
- URLs,
- GitHub issues,
- event pages,
- surveys,
- internal records,
- future publications.

The JP/EN label indicates the primary language of the session.

English sessions should not necessarily be simple translations of Japanese sessions. They can also explore differences in research culture, international laboratories, institutional norms, and AI adoption across countries or communities.

---

### 4.3 Research AI Forum

**Purpose:** Structured events with invited speakers or specialists.

The Forum is distinct from the Café.

While Café sessions are informal and peer-driven, Forums are more carefully organized around a specific question or theme and may include invited researchers, specialists, university staff, or external speakers.

Possible themes include:

- Experimental science × information science
- AI-generated code and scientific reproducibility
- Research data, intellectual property, and AI
- AI agents and automated research workflows
- What researchers need to know about machine learning
- The future of experimental research in an AI-rich environment
- How laboratories should establish internal AI practices

The Forum may include:

- invited talks,
- panel discussions,
- demonstrations,
- moderated Q&A,
- cross-disciplinary discussions.

Forum events use their own numbering system.

Examples:

- **Research AI Forum #001**
- **Research AI Forum #002**

---

### 4.4 Notes, Reports, and Resources

Events should not disappear after they end.

Whenever appropriate, the community should convert discussions into reusable public knowledge.

Possible outputs include:

- short event reports,
- anonymized discussion summaries,
- workflow collections,
- lists of useful tools,
- common concerns and failure modes,
- beginner guides,
- AI-use checklists,
- links to institutional policies,
- interviews,
- community surveys,
- longer synthesis reports.

These outputs may eventually form a **Research AI Commons knowledge base**.

---

## 5. The community feedback loop

The community is designed around a repeating cycle:

**Café → Questions → Forum → Reflection → Café → Publication**

### Step 1: Café

Researchers share real experiences and identify common problems.

### Step 2: Extract questions

The community identifies questions that cannot be answered through peer discussion alone.

For example:

- How should unpublished data be handled?
- How reliable is AI-generated analysis code?
- How much programming knowledge is necessary?
- What are the copyright implications of AI-generated figures?

### Step 3: Forum

Relevant experts or researchers are invited to address these questions.

### Step 4: Reflection

Participants discuss how the new information applies to actual laboratory practice.

### Step 5: Café

The community returns to an informal setting and asks:

**What should we actually change in our research workflows?**

### Step 6: Publication

Useful insights are summarized and shared publicly when appropriate.

This cycle is one of the core characteristics of Research AI Commons.

## 6. Initial target community

The initial activity is expected to grow from young experimental researchers, particularly graduate students and early-career researchers.

Potential early participants include:

- experimental biophysicists,
- molecular and cellular biologists,
- protein scientists,
- chemists,
- materials researchers,
- biomedical researchers,
- computational researchers working with experimental groups,
- researchers interested in AI-assisted workflows.

The initial network may include members of young-researcher communities such as the Hokkaido branch of the Biophysical Society of Japan’s young researchers network.

However, Research AI Commons should not be defined as a closed group for a single field or institution.

Its long-term identity should remain cross-disciplinary and expandable.

---

## 7. What makes this community different

Research AI Commons should not position itself simply as another “AI seminar.”

Its distinctive focus is:

### Real workflows

How AI is actually incorporated into daily research.

### Experimental research

Particular attention is paid to the parts of research that are difficult to capture through generic AI tutorials: experiments, samples, instruments, data quality, troubleshooting, tacit knowledge, and scientific judgment.

### Cross-lab exchange

Knowledge that would normally remain inside individual laboratories is discussed across research groups.

### Successes and failures

Failed workflows, incorrect outputs, wasted time, and uncertainty are legitimate discussion topics.

### Continuous community

The goal is not a single event, but repeated interaction.

### Café + Forum structure

Informal peer discussion and structured expert discussion complement each other.

### Japanese + English

The community is designed to include both Japanese-speaking and international researchers.

### Public knowledge creation

Useful lessons are gradually converted into open resources.

## 8. Principles for Café operation

### Keep participation easy

Café sessions should require little or no preparation from ordinary participants.

People should be welcome even if they:

- have never used AI for research,
- use only one AI service,
- have no programming experience,
- are unsure whether AI is useful for them.

### Avoid turning every Café into a lecture

A short lightning talk can help start discussion, but conversation should remain central.

### Encourage concrete examples

Whenever possible, discussions should move from abstract claims to real cases.

Instead of:

> “AI is useful for coding.”

Prefer:

> “I asked an AI tool to write a fitting routine for this dataset. It ran correctly, but the statistical model was inappropriate.”

### Normalize uncertainty

Participants should feel comfortable saying:

- “I do not know.”
- “I am not sure whether this is safe.”
- “This workflow did not work.”
- “I do not understand this tool.”
- “I tried it, but it made my work slower.”

### Produce a small outcome

Each Café should ideally leave behind at least one lightweight record:

- questions raised,
- recurring concerns,
- useful workflows,
- potential Forum topics,
- suggested future Café topics.

## 9. Data, confidentiality, and publication policy

Because discussions may involve active research, the community must handle information carefully.

### Participants should not be expected to disclose

- unpublished datasets,
- confidential experimental details,
- proprietary information,
- personal data,
- collaborator-confidential information,
- information restricted by institutional or contractual rules.

### Event organizers should

- clearly state whether sessions are recorded,
- avoid recording by default for small discussion-based events,
- obtain permission before publishing identifiable comments,
- anonymize discussion summaries when appropriate,
- distinguish personal opinions from institutional policy,
- avoid presenting informal discussion as formal scientific consensus.

### Public reports

Public reports should focus on transferable insights rather than identifiable details.

For example:

> “Several participants reported difficulty verifying AI-generated analysis code.”

is generally preferable to:

> “Researcher X from Laboratory Y said…”

unless explicit permission has been obtained.

---

## 10. Relationship with universities and other organizations

Research AI Commons may collaborate with universities, academic societies, laboratories, student groups, and external organizations.

However, unless formally approved, the community should not imply that it is an official organization of any university or institution.

The website and event materials should clearly distinguish:

- community-organized activities,
- institutional co-sponsored activities,
- official institutional policy.

If the community begins at Hokkaido University, this should be described as its origin or current activity base, not necessarily as an official university body.

---

## 11. Independence and neutrality

Research AI Commons should remain tool-neutral and company-neutral.

The community may discuss or demonstrate specific services such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, NotebookLM, Perplexity, or other tools, but the community itself should not become a promotional channel for a specific company.

When events are sponsored or supported by a company, organization, or program, that relationship should be disclosed clearly.

The value of the community comes from researchers being able to discuss both strengths and weaknesses openly.

---

## 12. Governance and succession

The community should be designed from the beginning so that it can continue even when the original organizer graduates or leaves.

### Recommended operational structure

Maintain community assets independently from any single member’s personal accounts wherever practical.

Core assets may include:

- a shared community email address,
- a GitHub Organization,
- the community website repository,
- event records,
- public reports,
- design assets,
- operation guides,
- survey templates,
- contact lists handled according to privacy rules.

### Roles

A lightweight role system is sufficient initially.

Possible roles:

**Coordinator**
- maintains overall direction,
- coordinates major decisions.

**Café organizer**
- schedules and facilitates Café sessions.

**Forum organizer**
- coordinates invited speakers and structured events.

**Web / archive maintainer**
- maintains the website, GitHub repository, and public resources.

**Community members**
- propose events, share workflows, and participate in discussions.

One person may initially hold multiple roles.

### Succession principle

No critical community asset should depend permanently on one individual.

Operational procedures should be documented so future members can take over.

### Google account

A dedicated community Google account may be useful for:

- shared email,
- Google Forms,
- surveys,
- event registration,
- Drive documents,
- calendars.

However, the account should not become the sole point of control.

Where possible:

- add multiple trusted administrators,
- use shared folders,
- document account recovery,
- avoid storing critical information only in one personal account.

## 17. Initial Café concept

The first Café can serve as a baseline discussion of current AI use among experimental researchers.

### Example title

**Research AI Café #001 | JP**

### Theme

**研究にAI、みんなどう使ってる？**  
実験系研究者のリアルなAI活用を持ち寄る座談会

### Goals

Identify:

- commonly used tools,
- commonly used workflows,
- successful use cases,
- failure modes,
- concerns,
- barriers,
- questions worth exploring in future Forums.

### Example structure

- 19:00–19:10 Opening
- 19:10–19:25 Current AI-use sharing
- 19:25–19:40 Lightning talks
- 19:40–20:10 Small-group discussion
- 20:10–20:30 Whole-group synthesis
- 20:30–21:00 Optional informal conversation

The most important output is not a conclusion.

It is a set of **real questions from researchers** that can guide future community activity.

## 18. Six-month development model

A possible first six months:

### Phase 1 — Start small

Hold the first Café with experimental researchers already connected through existing young-researcher networks.

### Phase 2 — Repeat

Run additional Café sessions in Japanese and English.

Observe recurring questions and barriers.

### Phase 3 — Expand across disciplines

Invite researchers from computational science, information science, engineering, and other fields.

### Phase 4 — Hold the first Forum

Select a question that emerged repeatedly from Café discussions and invite appropriate experts.

### Phase 5 — Reflect

Hold another Café after the Forum to discuss what participants would actually change in their research practice.

### Phase 6 — Publish

Produce a synthesis such as:

**How Young Researchers Are Actually Using AI in Research**

Possible contents:

- common AI workflows,
- successful examples,
- common failure modes,
- barriers to adoption,
- risk perceptions,
- disciplinary differences,
- recommendations for future discussion.

The goal is not to create a definitive AI manual.

The goal is to document how research practice is changing and help researchers learn from one another.

---

## 19. Long-term vision

Research AI Commons can grow in several directions without losing its core identity.

Possible future developments include:

- cross-university Café collaborations,
- national online Forums,
- researcher interviews,
- discipline-specific Café sessions,
- bilingual events,
- practical workflow demonstrations,
- annual community reports,
- collaborations with academic societies,
- open collections of researcher-tested workflows.

The community should grow only where there is genuine participant interest.

Scale itself is not the objective.

The objective is to create a durable culture in which researchers can openly exchange experience about emerging tools and critically examine how those tools are changing research.

## 22. Naming note

**Research AI Commons** is currently a working name.

The word **Commons** reflects the idea of a shared space where knowledge, experience, questions, and resources are contributed by the community rather than delivered in a one-way direction.

If the final name changes, the structure and principles in this document should remain usable.

Possible alternative naming directions previously considered include:

- Lab AI Commons
- Research AI Exchange
- Research Workflow Commons

The final name should ideally:

- remain usable beyond one university,
- remain usable beyond one research discipline,
- work in both Japanese and English,
- support Café / Forum sub-brands,
- avoid implying official university affiliation unless formally approved,
- remain meaningful even as specific AI tools change.

## 23. Guiding sentence for future decisions

When deciding whether a new event, collaboration, website feature, or resource belongs in this community, ask:

> **Does this help researchers share, examine, or improve real research workflows involving AI across labs or disciplines?**

If the answer is yes, it is likely aligned with the purpose of Research AI Commons.
